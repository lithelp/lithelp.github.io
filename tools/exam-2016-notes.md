# G.C.E. O/L 2016 – Appreciation of English Literary Texts (46-E, new syllabus)

Source: the paper and marking scheme PDFs in docs/resources/. Notes are transcribed from the scans for use in the study guides.

## Paper structure (3 hours, answer five questions)
- Part I, Q1 Section A: six extracts × 5 marks = 30. Each has (a) text and author, 1; (b) intra-textual reference such as speaker, addressee or situation, 2; (c) inference or response, 2.
- Part I, Q1 Section B: one novel passage, choose (a), (b) or (c). Questions: identification 2, comprehension 2, application (word meanings) 2, critical evaluation 4 = 10.
- Part II: four essays × 15 = 60, one each from Poetry, Drama, Prose and Fiction. Marked as Content 7, Organisation 4, Language 4.
- Content bands: 6–7 = all facts well selected, prioritised, logical, personal insight, creativity; 3–5 = most content, ordered, some personal response; 1–2 = some relevant facts, not prioritised.
- Organisation bands: 4 = precise, closely knit, quotations at the right moment, strong introduction, development and conclusion; 2–3 = focused, well ordered, quotations back up points; 1 = addresses the question but not cohesive; 0 = does not address the question.
- Language bands: 4 = no errors, distinctive style, rich vocabulary; 3 = no grammar errors, good vocabulary, a few spelling errors; 2 = no SVO errors, some tense, article or spelling errors; 1 = some SVO errors but communicates; 0 = cannot communicate.

## Section A extracts (2016)
1. (i) "The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls, / He watches from his mountain walls" – The Eagle. (a) where from, who wrote; (b) who is "he", where is he; (c) why does the sea crawl. Scheme page not available.
2. (ii) "We'll sweat no more on scythe and rake, / My bloody hands and I" – Farewell to Barn and Stack and Tree. (a) work and poet; (b) who are "we", what will they not do; (c) why bloody hands. Scheme page not available.
3. (iii) "…the chamberlain's nephew has sent me some real jewels and everybody knows that jewels cost more than flowers" – The Nightingale and the Rose, Oscar Wilde (1); the professor's daughter to the student (2); attitude to love: materialistic, money-minded, opportunistic, selfish (2).
4. (iv) "It is ok if I die…" – The Lahore Attack, Kumar Sangakkara (1); the soldier at the checkpoint to Sangakkara (2); respect, admiration, high regard, affection, appreciation (2).
5. (v) "I wished I might have lived with you for ever, setting up our world in the middle of this field, playing with children and working on the farm." – Twilight of a Crane, Junji Kinoshita (1); Tsu to Yohyo, or to herself, or to Yohyo in her mind (2); attitude to life: contented, moderate, unassuming, spiritual happiness, humble, simple (2).
6. (vi) "I absolutely like her. Absolutely! Even though her cheeks are dimpled, I like her. I am almost ready to let the debt go." – The Bear, Anton Chekhov (1); Smirnov about Popova (2); device: repetition, exaggeration or hyperbole, irony, sarcasm; effect: humour, irony, sarcasm, emphasis, suspense (2).

## Section B (2016)
- (a) Prince and the Pauper: "I will, I will indeed – nor ever touch another…batter it down myself betwixt midnight and the morning." Situation: Miles Hendon threatens the constable to expose his dishonest or secret action; the speaker is the constable (2). Promise: not to do the dishonest act again, to let the prisoner free (2). "Blind for my sake" = pretend not to see or ignore; "batter it down" = break or destroy the prison door (2). Feature of society: corruption, lawlessness, dishonesty of legal officers (4).
- (b) Bringing Tony Home: "And then came the bark…accordion in an arc just below his chin." Reunion with the dog on returning to Depanama to collect new ration books (and fulfil his secret vow to take Tony back), at the entrance to Mrs Jayasinghe's property (2). Tony excited because he meets the narrator after a period of absence (2). Delicately = carefully, skilfully, lightly, smoothly, mildly; suspended = hung loosely, held down (2). Relationship: loving, caring, intimate, close, genuine, affectionate (4).
- (c) Vendor of Sweets: "Mali proved unusually communicative…replaced by the blue airmail letters." Jagan starts getting letters from Mali after Mali has gone to America (2). Could not bind the letters because Mali left no margin (2). Epistles = important letters; speedy execution = quick performance (2). Jagan's character: love and affection for Mali, showing off and boastfulness, pretentiousness, hypocrisy, double standards towards the spiritual life (4).

## Part II essays (2016) – marking scheme points
- **Q2 To the Nile**: how Keats admires the service and beauty of the Nile (scheme page not available).
- **Q3 War is Kind**: "Stephen Crane ironically portrays the brutality caused by war." Brutality in how the soldiers die ("men were born to drill and die", "your lover threw wild hands toward the sky", "your father… raged at his breast"). The mother's passive yet heavy suffering ("Mother whose heart hung humble as a button"). Brainwashing of young soldiers, written sarcastically. Verbal irony ("Do not weep / war is kind"). Oxymorons ("virtue of slaughter", "excellence of killing"). Contrast: the battle-god's kingdom is full of corpses ("A field where a thousand corpses lie"). Glamour of war presented sarcastically ("Hoarse booming drums of the regiment"). The ironic title and its effect. Better answers: a cross-section of society affected (lover, father, son); the repetition of the title; how the glorification of war is turned upside down. Very good answers relate it to contemporary society.
- **Q4 I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings**: deprivation of freedom. About lack of freedom; the title shows the speaker understands the caged bird; the songs of the free and caged birds differ (comparison: "fearful trill" vs "dares to claim the sky"); the cage and clipped wings symbolise denial of freedom; lack of physical freedom and frustration ("His bars of rage"); lack of psychological freedom ("His shadow shouts on a nightmare scream"); contrast; what the free bird enjoys ("The fat worm waiting on a dawn-bright lawn and he names the sky his own"); repetition; anaphora ("and"). Better: freedom of people; the author's experience as a black woman (racism), female freedom.
- **Q5 The Clown's Wife**: personal vs professional life. Differences between his life as a clown and as a husband; the professional image ("up there he's a king on a throne"); his miserable life at home ("he seems to have the world on his shoulders"); the wife's feelings ("I do me best to cheer him up, poor soul"); the metaphor "king"; relate to public figures and to ordinary people playing roles. Better: reality vs appearance; entertainers may not live happy lives.
- **Q6 The Huntsman**: see the Huntsman guide.
- **Q7 Twilight of a Crane**: desire for money vs passion for love. Love: Tsu and Yohyo devoted ("Cold soup is no good for my sweet heart"); Yohyo refuses Sodo and Unzu at first ("I love Tsu…she is my darling"); Tsu loves his innocence and kindness ("You were once so innocent…I was deeply touched by it"); she weaves out of love, wants a peaceful life, is disappointed by the growing gap ("You are going farther and farther from me"); Yohyo reluctant to ask; Tsu risks her health; "never look in the weaving room"; her last sacrifice is two pieces of cloth; Yohyo runs aimlessly looking for her; love as the foundation of happiness. Money: Sodo and Unzu envious ("That lazy fellow has been coining money without lifting a finger over"); they recognise senba-ori; the two friends are not genuine with each other; Unzu scared because he cheated Yohyo; greed corrupts them; they bully Yohyo; Sodo asks him to coax, then threaten, his wife; Kyoto tempts him; greed wins ("Yes. I'll leave you, unless you weave the cloth"); Tsu notices ("You are moving to the other world where I can never live"); Yohyo devastated: he has money but not love.
- **Q8 The Bear**: significance of the title. "Bear" suggests aggressive, dominant, angry, hateful; Smirnov has no self-control, is driven by needs and desires, a symbol of vulgarity; his rough entry; harasses Luka ("You fool, you are too fond of talking"); insists on the money that day; insulting language ("Not one of the swine wants to pay me"); orders kvass; underestimates women ("That's really silly feminine logic", "I'd rather sit on a barrel of gun powder than talk to a woman"); invades and dominates the house; mimics Popova; orders oats for his horses; harasses Luka; breaks furniture; Popova calls him rude and ill-bred; speaks French to unsettle her; "a misfortune to be born a woman"; never seen faithful women (male chauvinism); Popova's abuse ("You're a boor! A coarse bear! A bourbon! a monster!"); she accepts the challenge.
- **Q9 The Lumber Room**: the aunt's character and the central theme. Brief plot and backdrop; central theme (generation gap, negative punishment by adults); the aunt represents adults, a self-styled conventional aunt with no imagination, does not understand children's need to explore, curtails freedom, obsessed with punishment, authoritarian, opportunistic, unsympathetic, stingy, uncaring, dishonest, stupid, uses religion to instil fear, exaggerates Nicholas's "disgrace". Quotations: "It was her habit whenever… the offender would be rigorously debarred"; "You are not to go into the gooseberry garden…"; "He told you twice, but you weren't listening. You often don't listen…"; "…privately resolving that Nicholas should have none of it"; "I told you not to but you now may".
- **Q10 The Nightingale and the Rose**: "Love is not half as useful as logic." State your position clearly; open-ended. Different attitudes to love: the Nightingale (love = sacrifice), the girl (material gains, gifts), the boy (emotion). Students tease out "useful" and "logic"; emotion vs reason in love. Better: the nature of women as represented (dishonesty); the boy's lack of logic though he talks of logic (thinks a rose wins love; never asks how the rose appeared; prejudice about the nightingale, "She would not sacrifice herself for others", disproved; generalises from one experience).
- **Q11 The Lahore Attack**: see the Lahore Attack guide.
- **Q12 Wave**: "The narrator reacts to the unexpected tragedy instinctively, rather than with reason." Introduce Sonali and the book; how the tragedy is faced; physical action and emotional tension ("the white curl of a big wave", "The foam turned into waves", "charging and churning", "Calls husband out of the bathroom"); she won't let her husband hold the children ("Give me one of them" Steve shouted. "But I didn't"); unaware of what they flee, she forgets to call her parents and accepts a lift from a stranger ("A man was driving the jeep. I didn't know who he was"); responds mechanically when Orlantha's mother Beulah falls. Better: compare with animal instincts (protecting young).
- **Q13 The Prince and the Pauper**: central theme. Good rewarded and evil punished; the change of roles a blessing to both Tom Canty and Edward Tudor (appearance vs reality); the contrast between rich and poor; miscarriage of justice; the ruling class unaware of the poor; stupidity of the ruling class; abuse of power; truthfulness rewarded; kingship; clothes matter; the power of heredity; the contrast between Henry VIII and Edward VI.
- **Q14 Vendor of Sweets**: tradition vs modernity in Jagan and Mali. Jagan sells sweets made traditionally; Mali doesn't want the business and wants to invest big; Jagan is a traditional Gandhian, versed in Hindu literature, spins his own cloth on a charka, strict vegetarian; Mali breaks away, goes to America, starts eating beef; prefers his college canteen to his father's food; Jagan sees marriage as sacred and lifelong, Mali sees it as business; Mali brings a half-Korean half-American woman and they are not married; Grace goes out at night; Mali becomes an addict (alcohol) and goes to prison; Jagan leaves home for spiritual solace.
- **Q15 Bringing Tony Home**: see the Bringing Tony Home guide.
