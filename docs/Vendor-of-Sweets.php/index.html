The Vendor of Sweets by R.K. Narayan

R.K. Narayan, Indian novelist writing in English

Setting

Main characters
Jagan, his son Mali, Grace, the cousin

About the author

R.K. Narayan is one of the best-known Indian novelists who wrote in English. Almost all his novels and stories are set in Malgudi, an imaginary town in South India that he created and returned to again and again. Earlier Malgudi novels include The Financial Expert and The Guide.

Narayan writes about ordinary people: shopkeepers, clerks, teachers and families. He is a gentle, ironic writer. He seldom tells us what to think. Instead, he shows the gap between what his characters say and what they do, and lets us smile at it. His humour is warm rather than cruel.

Remember

"Vendor of Sweets" is one of the three prescribed novels, with "The Prince and the Pauper" and "Bringing Tony Home". In the exam, novels appear in Part I, Section B (a passage with four questions, 10 marks) and Part II, Fiction (an essay, 15 marks).

Background

To understand Jagan you need to know three things about the India he lives in:

  • Gandhi and the freedom movement. As a young man Jagan followed Mahatma Gandhi. He joined the struggle against British rule, went to prison, and promised Gandhi that he would spin cloth every day. He still spins his own yarn and wears khadi (hand-spun cloth).
  • The Bhagavad Gita. Jagan reads this Hindu holy book every day, even in his shop. It teaches that a man should grow detached from worldly things and, in later life, withdraw from the world. In Hindu tradition this later stage is the Vanaprastha (retreat into the forest), which comes before Sanyas, complete renunciation.
  • The pull of the West. In the years after independence, many young Indians were fascinated by America: its money, its machines and its freedom. Mali represents this new generation.

The original LitHelp notes sum up the two sides neatly: Jagan represents "the superficial aspects of the East and Mali the weaker aspects of the West". Neither of them is perfect, and Narayan treats both with irony.

Setting

The whole novel takes place in Malgudi, a district town in South India with its own court, jail, college and busy streets. Places that matter in the story:

  • Jagan's sweet shop on Market Road: a crowded street near a school. Jagan sits on his gaddi (seat) with the Gita open, supervising four cooks and the boy at the counter.
  • Jagan's ancestral house: once full of relatives and lamps, now empty except for Jagan and Mali. Later he seals off his part of it.
  • The Lawley Statue: where young men gather to chat, Mali among them, towering over his friends.
  • The garden across the river: a quiet, wild place with a pond, bushes and hills, where a sculptor is carving a goddess. It becomes Jagan's "retreat".

Malgudi is changing. New suburbs are growing and modern ways are arriving, but old customs, caste rules and family pressures remain strong. In this sense Malgudi is a small picture of the whole of India: tradition and modernity side by side.

Plot

The story is told mostly in the order it happens, but one chapter (chapter twelve) goes back in time to tell the story of Jagan's marriage and Mali's birth.

  1. Jagan's peaceful routineJagan, a widower nearly sixty, runs a successful sweet shop, sells "pure stuff" made with real ghee, reads the Gita and follows Gandhian rules about food, clothes and simple living. He lives alone with his son Mali, whom he adores.
  2. The past (told in flashback)Jagan married Ambika. After ten years without a child they made a pilgrimage to the temple of Santana Krishna, and Mali was born. Ambika later died of a brain tumour. Jagan believed in "nature cure", and Mali grew up feeling that his father's theories had killed his mother. A silent barrier has stood between father and son ever since.
  3. Mali leaves collegeMali suddenly stops studying and announces that he wants to be a writer. He has seen a competition in a magazine offering a prize of twenty-five thousand rupees for a novel.
  4. Mali goes to AmericaInstead of writing, Mali secretly arranges a passport and takes money from the hoard Jagan keeps hidden in the loft. He flies to America to learn "novel-writing". Jagan is shocked but secretly proud.
  5. The lettersMali's blue airmail letters arrive from America. Jagan carries them in his pocket and reads them aloud to anyone who will listen. Gradually, they replace his reading of the Gita (the 2016 exam passage).
  6. Mali returns with GraceMali comes back with Grace, a young woman who is half-Korean and half-American, and introduces her as his wife. Grace tries hard to become "an Indian daughter-in-law", cleaning and caring for Jagan's part of the house. Because she is a Christian, Jagan's relatives cast him out.
  7. The story-writing machineMali wants to set up a factory to make a machine that writes stories, with an American company. He demands over two lakh rupees from his father. Jagan refuses.
  8. "Not married"Jagan learns that Mali and Grace are not married at all. To him, his house has become polluted. Mali wants Grace sent back to America.
  9. Non-violent non-cooperationJagan responds in Gandhi's way. He seals off his part of the house and uses the back door, offers Mali the shop, and cuts the price of his sweets to a quarter, which causes panic among the other sweet-sellers.
  10. The retreatChinna Dorai, a sculptor who now dyes hair, shows Jagan a garden across the river. Jagan decides to leave his worldly life and live there, entering the Vanaprastha stage. He leaves the shop to his cousin.
  11. Mali in jailAs Jagan leaves, the cousin tells him that Mali has been arrested for having a bottle of alcohol (wine) with him. Jagan gives the cousin a cheque for Mali's bail, although he thinks "a dose of prison-life would be good for him", and he is ready to pay Grace's fare home because "she is a good girl". Then he goes to his retreat, taking his spinning wheel and his cheque book.

Characters

JJagan
The vendor of sweets and central character. A devoted follower of Gandhi and reader of the Gita who spins his own cloth, cooks his own food and wears sandals made only from the leather of animals that died naturally. Yet he is also a shrewd businessman who keeps a second, untaxed account. He loves his son deeply but is too gentle and too fond to stand up to him. Neither a simple hypocrite nor a saint: a mixture of strength and weakness, tradition and modernity.
MMali
Jagan's only son: tall, confident and persuasive. Spoilt by a doting father, he speaks to Jagan in short, clipped sentences and takes his love for granted. He rejects his father's world: college, Indian customs, vegetarian food and traditional marriage. He takes his father's money, goes to America and returns with grand plans. He ends in jail.
GGrace
Mali's half-Korean, half-American companion. Warm, hard-working and eager to please, she tries to live like an Indian daughter-in-law, calling Jagan "Father" and cleaning his rooms. Jagan likes her. She is caught between father and son and becomes a victim of Mali's selfishness.
CThe cousin
Never given a name; everyone calls him "cousin" except Mali, who calls him "uncle". A man about town who knows everyone. He visits the shop every afternoon, tastes the sweets, carries news and gives advice. His simple, sensible questions expose the contradictions in Jagan's behaviour.
AAmbika
Jagan's dead wife, seen only in memory and in the flashback. Proud and spirited, she stood up to her mother-in-law. Her death still shapes the relationship between father and son.
DChinna Dorai
A bearded sculptor who now earns his living as a hair-dyer. He leads Jagan to the garden across the river and the idea of retreat.

Detailed analysis

Jagan: a man of contradictions

The novel opens with Jagan telling his cousin, "Conquer taste, and you will have conquered the self." When the cousin asks, "Why conquer the self?", Jagan can only reply, "I do not know, but all our sages advise us so." Narayan's irony is already at work: the man preaching against taste earns his living by selling sweets. The cousin later says, "I cannot understand why you go on working and earning, taking all this trouble."

Jagan's money habits show the same contradiction. The cash that came in after six o'clock went into a separate notebook. Narayan describes how Jagan "viewed it as free cash, whatever that might mean, a sort of immaculate conception, self-generated, arising out of itself and entitled to survive without reference to any tax". The joke lies in the solemn, almost religious language used for a simple tax dodge.

Yet Jagan is not a mere hypocrite. He truly suffered for Gandhi, keeps his promise to spin, refuses to adulterate his sweets, and lives very simply. Even his unusual ideas ("Do we engage people to breathe for us?" he asks, when explaining why he cooks for himself) come from real conviction. His weaknesses are the ordinary weaknesses of a human being.

Father and son

Jagan loves Mali without limit. When he discovers that Mali has taken money from the loft to go to America, his first reaction is not anger but admiration: "The boy is very practical", and "See how self-reliant he has grown." This blind affection is the root of the problem. Mali never learns to respect his father, and Jagan never learns to say no until it is too late.

The silent barrier between them goes back to Ambika's death. Mali believes his father's "nature cure" ideas killed his mother, and he keeps his father at a distance. Their conversations are short, awkward and full of misunderstanding. The 2016 exam passage shows Jagan building a whole world out of Mali's letters, reading them aloud to anyone, while Mali himself "sounded brusque, disconnected, or impersonal".

The story-writing machine

Mali's machine is the novel's great symbol of modern, Western thinking taken too far. Mali tells his father, "With this machine anyone can write a story. Come nearer, and you will see how it works." To Mali, a story is just a matter of combining characters, situations, emotions and a climax. To Jagan, and to Narayan, art cannot be produced by a machine. The machine also represents Mali's belief that money and technology can replace effort, talent and tradition.

Grace

Grace is the most sympathetic character in the novel. She tells Jagan, "Father, you think I mind it? I don't; I must not forget that I'm an Indian daughter-in-law." She learns to wear a sari and cleans Jagan's kitchen. Jagan grows fond of her. Their conversation about caste is gently comic: when Jagan says, "Gandhi fought for its abolition," Grace asks innocently, "Is it gone now?" and Jagan answers like a politician, "It's going." Grace shows that East and West can live together with kindness, which makes her treatment by Mali all the sadder.

Tradition and the family

Jagan's relatives turn against him because a Christian woman lives in his house. His sister writes, "We are ashamed to refer to you as a brother." For Jagan, the discovery that Mali and Grace are not married is far worse. His house seems polluted, and he cannot bear to live in it as before.

The ending

Jagan's answer to his crisis is a Gandhian one: non-violent non-cooperation, followed by withdrawal from the world. But Narayan keeps his irony to the end. Jagan goes to his retreat carrying both his spinning wheel and his cheque book, and he arranges for the cousin to run the shop and send him accounts. He enters only the Vanaprastha stage, not full renunciation. Meanwhile, Mali sits in jail. The ending is open: we never learn what becomes of Mali or Grace.

Themes

  • Tradition versus modernityThe central theme. Jagan's Gandhian, Hindu, vegetarian, Indian way of life clashes with Mali's American ideas about money, food, marriage and success.
  • East and WestIndia and America meet through Mali and Grace. Narayan shows the weaknesses of both sides, but also, through Grace, that they can meet with kindness.
  • The generation gapA loving father and a selfish son who cannot understand each other. Neither sees the other's point of view.
  • Appearance and realityJagan's saintly talk and his business practices; Mali's grand plans and his empty achievements; a "marriage" that is not a marriage.
  • Parental loveJagan's love for Mali is deep but blind, and it spoils the son it is meant to help.
  • Detachment and renunciationJagan's slow, imperfect journey from worldly life towards the spiritual retreat that the Gita describes.
  • MoneyMoney causes the conflict: the hidden hoard, the stolen money, the two-lakh demand, the price cut. Jagan finally decides that his wealth is at the root of his troubles.

Passage questions (Part I, Section B)

Exam format

Section B gives a passage from a novel with four questions: (i) identification of the situation, 2 marks; (ii) comprehension, 2 marks; (iii) application, explaining words or expressions, 2 marks; (iv) critical evaluation, 4 marks. Total 10 marks.

Official past paper · G.C.E. O/L 2016, Q1 Section B (c)
"Mali proved unusually communicative from across the seas, and although at times he sounded brusque, disconnected, or impersonal, he generalized a good deal about the civilization in which he found himself. The blue airmail letters grew into a file. If only Mali had taken the precaution of leaving a proper margin to his epistles, Jagan would have bound them into a neat little volume at Truth Printing; surely Nataraj would have realized its importance and obliged him with a speedy execution. Jagan stuffed his jibba pocket with the letters and pulled them out for choice reading of passages to all and sundry, mostly to his cousin, who, as ever, remained an uncomplaining listener. Gradually his reading of the Bhagavad Gita was replaced by the blue airmail letters."
  1. What is the situation given in the passage? When does it take place? 2
  2. Why couldn't Jagan bind the letters into a neat little volume? 2
  3. Write the meanings of the following expressions: (a) epistles (b) speedy execution 2
  4. What aspects of Jagan's character are revealed by the passage? 4
Show answers (based on the 2016 marking scheme)

(i) Jagan starts receiving letters from his son Mali. It takes place after Mali has gone to America; Jagan discusses the letters with his cousin.

(ii) Because Mali did not leave a proper margin when he wrote his letters, so they could not be bound.

(iii) (a) epistles: letters (important letters). (b) speedy execution: doing the job quickly.

(iv) The scheme gives 4 marks for more than two of these points: Jagan's love and affection for Mali (he treasures every letter and wants to bind them like a book); his showing off and boastfulness (he reads them aloud "to all and sundry"); his pretentiousness; his hypocritical attitude; and his double standards towards the spiritual life (the letters gradually replace his reading of the Bhagavad Gita, the book he claims guides his life).

Practice passage · LitHelp
"In his small notebook he entered only the cash that came in after six o'clock, out of the smaller jug. This cash was in an independent category, he viewed it as free cash, whatever that might mean, a sort of immaculate conception, self-generated, arising out of itself and entitled to survive without reference to any tax. It was converted into crisp currency at the earliest moment, tied into a bundle and put away in the loft at home."
  1. What is the situation in the passage? Where does it take place? 2
  2. What did Jagan do with the "free cash"? 2
  3. Explain the following expressions: (a) free cash (b) without reference to any tax 2
  4. What does this passage reveal about Jagan's character? How does Narayan use irony here? 4
Show answers

(i) At the end of the day Jagan counts the money collected in his sweet shop. The passage describes how he keeps the cash that came in after six o'clock separately. It takes place in his shop on Market Road.

(ii) He changed it into notes, tied it into a bundle and hid it in the loft of his house.

(iii) (a) free cash: money he treats as belonging to no one's account, free of any duty. (b) without reference to any tax: without paying tax on it, without showing it to the tax authorities.

(iv) Jagan is a shrewd, money-minded businessman who cheats the tax department, even though he claims to follow Gandhi and the Gita. The irony lies in the solemn, almost religious words ("immaculate conception", "self-generated") used for a simple tax dodge, and in the "whatever that might mean", which shows that even Jagan cannot explain it. The hidden money is also important to the plot: it is the money Mali later takes to go to America.

Essay questions (Part II, Fiction)

How essays are marked (15 marks)

Content 7 · Organisation 4 · Language 4. Refer to specific events in the novel and quote where you can.

Official past paper · G.C.E. O/L 2016, Q14

"The conflict between tradition and modernity is symbolized through the relationship between Jagan and Mali in The Vendor of Sweets." Discuss referring to the events of the novel. 15

Points the 2016 marking scheme looks for
  • Jagan, a seller of sweets, uses traditional ingredients in a traditional way.
  • Mali does not want to take over his father's business. He wants to act at once and invest money in business in large amounts.
  • Jagan is a traditional Indian: he follows the principles of Gandhi, is well versed in Hindu literature and cultural practices, spins cloth for himself on a charkha, and is a strict vegetarian.
  • Mali breaks away from Indian traditions and goes to America. He appreciates American culture and starts eating beef.
  • Mali prefers food at his college canteen to food made by his father.
  • Jagan thinks marriage is sacred and lifelong, and believes in traditional Hindu marriage rituals. Mali thinks it is a business matter. He brings home a half-Korean, half-American woman, and they are not even married.
  • Grace goes out at night; in Jagan's world women do not go out alone at night.
  • Mali becomes addicted to alcohol and goes to prison.
  • Jagan leaves home, finding solace in spiritual practices.
Show model answer

R.K. Narayan's "The Vendor of Sweets" is the story of Jagan, a sweet-seller of Malgudi, and his only son Mali. On the surface it is a family story, but the clash between father and son also stands for a larger conflict in modern India: the conflict between tradition and modernity. I agree that their relationship symbolises this conflict.

Jagan represents tradition. He is a follower of Gandhi who spins his own cloth on the charkha, wears khadi, cooks his own food and eats no meat. He reads the Bhagavad Gita every day in his shop. Even his business is traditional: he makes his sweets with pure ghee in the old way and is proud of selling "pure stuff". His values are simplicity, self-control and duty, summed up in his favourite saying, "Conquer taste, and you will have conquered the self."

Mali represents modernity, or at least its weaker side. He has no interest in his father's shop or in college. He wants quick success and large sums of money. He takes his father's hidden savings and goes to America, where he learns to admire American culture and even eats beef. He returns with a plan to build a story-writing machine with an American company, and demands over two lakh rupees from his father. For Mali, even art is a matter of machines and money.

The conflict is sharpest over marriage. For Jagan, marriage is sacred and lifelong, celebrated with traditional Hindu rituals, as the flashback of his own wedding to Ambika shows. Mali brings home Grace, a half-Korean, half-American woman, as his "wife", and later it turns out they are not married at all. To Jagan this is a shock: his house feels polluted, and his relatives cast him out. Grace's freedom, such as going out alone at night, also shocks Jagan's traditional world.

Neither side wins. Mali's modern dreams end in jail when he is caught with alcohol. Jagan cannot stop his son by force, so he answers in Gandhi's way, with non-violent non-cooperation, and finally withdraws to a retreat across the river to find peace in spiritual life. Narayan does not present tradition as perfect, though: Jagan cheats the tax department and takes his cheque book to his retreat. The novel suggests that the conflict between old and new is real and painful, and that both sides have their faults.

In conclusion, the relationship between Jagan and Mali is much more than a quarrel between a father and son. Through the charkha and the story-writing machine, the Gita and the airmail letters, Narayan symbolises the conflict between India's traditions and the attractions of modern Western life.

Practice question · LitHelp

"Jagan is not a hypocrite, but a mixture of strength and weakness." Do you agree? 15

Show answer plan
  • Introduction: Jagan's beliefs (Gandhi, the Gita) and his life as a businessman seem to contradict each other; state your position.
  • Signs of hypocrisy: preaching "Conquer taste" while selling sweets; the untaxed "free cash"; boasting about Mali's letters while the Gita lies forgotten.
  • Signs of sincerity: imprisonment for Gandhi's cause; daily spinning; pure ingredients; simple food; leather only from animals that died naturally.
  • Weakness as a father: blind love, admiration even when Mali steals, inability to say no.
  • Strength at the end: refusing the two lakh rupees; non-violent non-cooperation; choosing the retreat.
  • Narayan's irony: the cheque book in the retreat; he enters only the Vanaprastha stage.
  • Conclusion: an ordinary, likeable man whose ideals and weaknesses exist side by side.
Practice question · LitHelp

Discuss the role of the cousin in "The Vendor of Sweets". 15

Show answer plan
  • Introduction: an unnamed character known to everyone in Malgudi; "uncle" only to Mali.
  • Jagan's listener and adviser: visits the shop every afternoon; the "uncomplaining listener" to Mali's letters.
  • The voice of common sense: his questions, such as "I cannot understand why you go on working and earning", expose Jagan's contradictions and create humour.
  • Carrier of news: finds out Mali's plans; warns Jagan that rival sweet-sellers may buy his cheap sweets.
  • A link between characters: speaks to Mali when Jagan cannot.
  • At the end: takes over the shop and arranges Mali's bail.
  • Conclusion: a small but essential character who carries much of Narayan's irony.
Common mistakes
  • Calling Jagan simply a "hypocrite". Better answers show both his sincerity and his contradictions.
  • Saying Mali and Grace were married. Mali introduces her as his wife, but they are not married.
  • Forgetting the irony of the ending: Jagan goes to his retreat with his cheque book.
  • Retelling the whole plot. Choose the events that answer the question.
  • Mixing up Narayan's Malgudi with a real town. Malgudi is imaginary.
